How we’ll measure

Proposed checks

Check the work and the result.

For a GTM lead, a useful launch program manager should help people reach decisions and deliver an agreed plan as scope, positioning and dates change. These are the questions we plan to test.

Coordinated is prelaunch, with no launched users or customer results. Any future pilot needs separately agreed scope, permissions and readiness. We would agree the starting point, owners and review dates before evaluating it.

Six questions

01

How much human effort did it take?

Count everyone’s time: setup, answering questions, making decisions, checking evidence, correcting mistakes and following through. Include any work our team does by hand, such as mapping dependencies or chasing owners.

02

Did decisions happen in time?

Check whether missing owners, dependencies and approvals surfaced while people could still act. Compare decision dates with the lead times owners confirmed. Keep late decisions and unanswered questions in the result.

03

Did owners confirm what they could deliver?

After a scope or date decision, ask each affected owner what they can deliver and by when. Check whether the plan reflects those answers. A message being seen is not a confirmed commitment.

04

Were approved changes delivered?

Check the actual materials, product scope and other affected work against the approved plan. Count missed changes, conflicting claims and corrections. Keep required release and security gates visible.

05

What work could people stop doing?

Ask which setup, chasing, status updates or reviews people no longer needed, and which remained. Count new setup and correction work too. Compare the same launch work and constraints on both sides.

06

What were the results and unanswered questions?

Check delivery separately from the intended business result, using an agreed measure, source, owner and review date. Report missing or inconclusive evidence. People decide what to change next and who owns the next check.

The approach

Agree the comparison before the test.

Write down the launch scope, starting point, measures and review dates with the relevant owners. Preserve that starting point if the plan changes. Label manual work and prototype behavior clearly, and report limitations alongside observations.

A before-and-after change alone does not prove what caused it. We will not present an illustrative example as a measured saving.
